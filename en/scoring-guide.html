A practical scoring guide

Equal averages can hide very different choices

The goal of a good rating table is not simply to produce the highest number. Readers should understand what each score means, which criteria matter, and how the conclusion was reached. This walkthrough uses three fictional trip plans to illustrate ties, weighting, and incomplete data. It is not a comparison of actual offers or travel costs.

1. Define your scoring rubric first

Write down the decision you are trying to make. Score every option using the same criteria and the same ten-point scale: enjoyment, affordability, and ease of travel. For every criterion, a higher number means better for the person making the choice. Affordability therefore means fitting the budget comfortably, not a high price. These are example anchors, not measured claims.

2. Show source scores, not just an average

Imagine three trip plans named A, B, and C. Publishing only a total score would conceal useful trade-offs, so show the source criteria alongside the result.

Values are rounded to two decimal places. Plans A and B tie on the equal-weight average, but A offers more enjoyment while B is more affordable. If strengths and weaknesses matter, a radar chart can communicate more than an overall rank alone.

3. Weighting changes the decision

With an unweighted average, all criteria have weight 1:1:1. To double the importance of enjoyment, multiply that score by two, add the other scores, and divide by four. Plan A is (9×2 + 5 + 8) ÷ 4 = 7.75. Plan B is (7×2 + 8 + 7) ÷ 4 = 7.25.

Now prioritize the budget with weights 1:3:1. Plan A becomes (9 + 5×3 + 8) ÷ 5 = 6.40; B becomes (7 + 8×3 + 7) ÷ 5 = 7.60; and C becomes (6 + 9×3 + 6) ÷ 5 = 7.80. Plan C now ranks first. The arithmetic is consistent: the priorities changed.

Document your priorities before scoring rather than selecting weights after seeing which option wins. Readers should be able to reproduce the calculation from the published criteria and weights.

4. Keep missing ratings distinct from zero

An empty rating is not an intentional score of zero. If you do not know a value, leave it as missing and explain the limitation, or collect the same information for every option before ranking. Verify how the particular scoring mode or chart handles incomplete entries before sharing a result.

A tie can still conceal meaningful differences. You can retain tied ranks or establish a tie-breaker in advance; do not alter input values merely to force a winner.

If several people contribute ratings, agree on what the scale means. Label personal opinions as opinions, and distinguish them from verified measurements such as price and travel time.

5. Reproduce the comparison in Stats Maker

  1. On the Home scoring sheet, add three subjects (Plans A, B, C) and three criteria (Enjoyment, Affordability, Ease of travel).
  2. Choose the 10-point scoring mode and enter the values from the table. When switching the scale on an existing sheet, check the app's score-conversion behavior.
  3. Compare the equal-weight results first. If you use the weighting controls, try 2:1:1 and 1:3:1 and inspect how the results change.
  4. Pick Radar Chart to compare strengths, Ranking Card to show overall positions, or Tier List to group options. An exported PNG is distinct from the editable project data.
  5. Before sharing, label the scores as fictional, state the scoring scale and weights, and distinguish judgment calls from facts. You can export an image without publishing the project.
