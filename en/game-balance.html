ORIGINAL INTERACTIVE CASE STUDY

Balance five game weapons without inventing a single best answer

How do you compare a fast dagger with a heavy hammer or defensive shield? Use a fully disclosed, imaginary data set to test whether the best choice changes when speed, defense or attack matters more. These values are original illustrations, not facts from any game.

The question behind the ranking

A single total hides trade-offs. The dagger is fast but fragile; the shield is defensive but slow. Instead of pretending that every player wants the same gear, start with a shared scoring rubric and change the priority weights openly. The method works for original game prototypes, board games or classroom exercises.

Scoring rubric (0–100)

All five items use the same four criteria and the same higher-is-better scale. These fictional scores reflect one transparent design exercise; there are no hidden accuracy claims.

  • Damage: how much pressure it creates on the target in our imagined encounter.
  • Speed: frequency of opportunities to act, not movement speed.
  • Defense: protection or safety margin during the encounter.
  • Control: ability to steer position and choices with predictable outcomes.

What the scenarios reveal

  1. Equal weights (1:1:1:1): Azure Spear leads at 71.25. Its scores are reasonably balanced; it is not the best in any single criterion.
  2. Speed focus (1:3:0:1): Wind Dagger leads at 83.20, ahead of Moon Bow at 75.80. The ranking changes because the priority changes, not because the source scores were altered.
  3. Defense focus (1:0:3:1): Iron Shield leads at 84.20. A high protection rating can outweigh its weaker damage and speed.
  4. Damage focus (4:1:0:0): Stone Hammer leads at 82.40, narrowly ahead of Azure Spear at 80.80. A small lead should not be mistaken for a robust design verdict.

The model ignores attack timing, stamina, costs, matchmaking, level progression and actual play-test results. It should not be used to declare a weapon objectively overpowered. For real balancing, capture comparable play-test observations, segment players, repeat measurements and explain uncertainty.

What this comparison does not prove

Common interpretation mistakes

Why not just use total points?

Total points silently assigns equal weights and can hide that two very different weapons receive the same overall number.

Are the scores objective game facts?

No. Every item and score in this page was invented as an instructional example. They are not measured weapon statistics or player research.
