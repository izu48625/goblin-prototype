Three ways to visualize the same scores

One rating sheet can tell several different stories. The three graphics here are exported using Stats Maker's own chart renderer and use the same hypothetical team ratings. All teams and numbers are editorial examples, not verified sports measurements.

One scoring method for all three charts

Six example teams are rated out of 100 for Attack, Defense, Speed, and Teamwork. These are illustrative opinions, not sports statistics. We apply the same four criteria to all teams, without weighting. Overall scores are calculated as the simple arithmetic mean of those four ratings.

TeamAttackDefenseSpeedTeamworkAverage
Green9590959092.5
Purple8085908083.75
Red9060709077.5
Blue6090807075
Yellow5560656060
Gray4050554547.5

Green's average is (95 + 90 + 95 + 90) ÷ 4 = 92.5. Showing the original numbers and method helps readers check the ranking themselves. The native graphic rounds decimal values to one place: Purple's 83.75 appears as 83.8 in the exported image. The table above retains the unrounded score.

Compare multiple strengths

Example 1: A radar chart shows different strengths

Red scores Attack 90, Defense 60, Speed 70, and Teamwork 90. Blue scores Attack 60, Defense 90, Speed 80, and Teamwork 70. Red leads in Attack and Teamwork while Blue leads in Defense and Speed, so the chart polygons spread in different directions.

A larger-looking polygon is not sufficient evidence of overall superiority. Axis order and scoring scale affect the shape. Show criteria and underlying values whenever possible.

Build it in Stats Maker

Choose Radar Chart from Visual Tools, set up four axes, and enter both series. Use a radar chart when the profile of strengths matters more than a single overall rank.

Communicate order and scores

Example 2: A ranking card is clear about overall order

Green ranks first with 92.5, Purple second with 83.75, Red third with 77.5, and Blue fourth with 75. A ranking card communicates order and gaps within one shareable graphic.

But it hides a key detail: Red performs worse in Defense than Blue, despite its higher average. If the reader needs that context, link to the original data or a radar chart. Mark subjective ratings as opinions, not real-world measurements.

Build it in Stats Maker

Score each criterion on the rating sheet, calculate the average for your desired ranking, and open Ranking Card. This example enters the derived mean ratings rather than presenting them as independent measurements.

Group similar ratings

Example 3: A tier list groups similar scores

The thresholds are S = 90–100, A = 80–89, B = 70–79, C = 60–69, and D = below 60. That places Green in S, Purple in A, Red and Blue together in B, Yellow in C, and Gray in D.

Red and Blue both belong to B, but their averages still differ (77.5 and 75). A tier is a category, not proof of a tie. If exact score differences matter, use a ranking chart instead.

Build it in Stats Maker

Choose Tier List from Visual Tools. Enter items and their scores, and define the S–D boundaries. Explain the thresholds to your readers when they are not obvious.
